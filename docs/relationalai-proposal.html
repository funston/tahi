The experiment

Take a benchmark where the knowledge graph is the dataset's ground truth, so extraction quality is never a variable. Answers are entity lists, so scoring is exact set equality and no LLM sits in judgement. Then write the same knowledge out as sentences and give that to a dense retriever.

The text arm and the graph arm now hold identical information. The only difference between them is access method — similarity search against traversal. Anything that moves is attributable to that and nothing else.

Three places the graph can act, tested separately on the same 100 held-out questions, same model, same seed.

McNemar exact, two-sided, on the discordant cells: p < 0.0001 every row. Post- and pre-generation use gpt-4o-mini with BGE-large-en-v1.5. In-generation is a local Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, where the constraint overrode the model's own top choice on 44% of decode steps.

The column that matters is broken. Zero, in every arm, on two models, under both query-derivation methods. A corrector that fixes some answers and breaks others cannot be deployed where the answer matters, because nothing tells you which happened to any single answer. At zero, the worst case for any answer is “no better than before”.

Two findings that outlive the benchmark

Injection point beat retrieval, four to one. Identical graph, identical query, identical model, identical candidates: 0.18 pasted into the prompt, 0.79 handed back as a diff of what was provably wrong. Prompt-stuffing a knowledge graph is the common integration and the weakest of the three by a wide margin.

The model ends up transcribing, not reasoning. End-to-end accuracy (0.790) equals the traversal's own exact-match rate (0.790), to three decimals. The correction loop drives the model to recite the supported set — so graph precision is the score. Improve the traversal and the number moves; change the LLM and it likely does not.

What this does not show

Stated at the same volume as the result, because these are what a reviewer finds in ten minutes.

The graph was free MetaQA's graph is dataset ground truth. Extraction quality was never tested — and our own extraction on enterprise prose scored −0.12 against its own source documents.
Traversal recall is 0.790 On 21% of questions the traversal cannot reach a gold answer at all. A hard ceiling that no amount of prompting recovers.
Nine relations Deriving a query by enumeration is O(|R|³). It does not survive a schema with hundreds of relation types.
No entity linking The subject entity is supplied by the dataset in every arm.
Weak baseline, one seed RAG scores 2%. Every figure is seed 0, n = 100. Two weak systems compared, not a strong baseline against a stronger one.
Far from the ceiling Published MetaQA 3-hop: GraftNet 77.7 · PullNet 91.4 · EmbedKGQA 94.8.

The first limit is the whole reason for this conversation. The condition that makes these numbers clean — a maintained, resolved, governed knowledge graph — is the condition RelationalAI's customers already live in permanently.

Some ideas worth exploring

None of this is a pitch — it's the list I'd bring to a whiteboard. Every one of them is a measurement first: run it on real data, look at the number, then decide whether there's anything there. Roughly in order of how well it fits what Rel already does.

1  ·  Automate the loop you already describe

Your own page says “if Rel gets something wrong, you fix it.” That's a human catching the error. This is the same loop with the semantic model doing the catching — generate, query the model, diff, hand back only what's provably wrong, ask again. I'm not proposing a new idea here. It's the thing you already designed, without the human in it.

2  ·  The number Spider 2.0 can't give you

A leaderboard result is a public-benchmark number. Nobody at AT&T runs Spider — they want the error rate on their schema, and sooner or later somebody in risk asks for it in writing. A per-customer eval harness would answer that: paired arms, held-out questions, McNemar exact, fixed and broken counts, pass conditions written down before the run.

Honestly this is the piece of my work that's furthest along — the measurement scaffolding is more mature than the mechanism it was built to measure.

3  ·  Wanting to be right vs. being unable to be wrong

Fine-tuning shifts probabilities. It makes fabrication unlikely, never impossible. Masking the sampler makes an entity that isn't in the semantic model unreachable — the decoder physically cannot emit it. Those are different guarantees and they stack; I'd never argue for one instead of the other.

What it buys you is a sentence you can't currently write: the agent cannot name a supplier, account or counterparty that isn't in your governed data.

4  ·  A hunch about Open Telco

Half-formed, but it nags at me. Open Telco is a serious body of work — 31 fine-tuned models across the whole stack, embedders, rerankers, LLMs, safety variants that abstain when context looks thin. Trained on 326k examples curated by a hundred-plus domain experts out of 3GPP, O-RAN, IETF and GSMA. Reranker MRR@10 0.952. Nothing about it is sloppy.

Two things caught my eye, neither of which is a criticism.

There's no structured layer anywhere in it — their own summary says the system “relies on retrieved passage context rather than structured semantic representations.” Which is odd, because 3GPP and O-RAN are about as structured as a corpus gets: releases, procedures, interfaces, parameter identifiers, and formal relationships between all of them. It reads like a semantic model waiting to happen. Strange thing to say to you, given that's your business and AT&T's already your customer.

And under their own limitations: “generated content requires verification before operational use.” Same sentence, essentially, as “if Rel gets something wrong, you fix it.” Both of you landed on a human doing the checking.

The hunch: with a reranker that good, the retrieval is fine — so if answers are still wrong, the failure has moved downstream of ranking. I'd want to know how often a generated answer names a spec, release, interface or parameter that appears in none of the retrieved passages. If that number is meaningfully above zero, no amount of further reranking touches it, because it's a decode-time thing. Their datasets are public, so it's an afternoon to find out. Might be nothing. Might be the whole story.

5  ·  Legend, where this might just fall out

I gather the thinking on the Legend side has been to train or fine-tune something to learn Pure and do text-to-Legend. Worth asking whether that's the hard way round.

Everything that made my results caveat-heavy was about the graph — where does it come from, is it trustworthy, what did extraction cost us. With Legend, the graph is already there and it's formal. A Legend model is a typed class/property/association graph, version-controlled through SDLC. Nothing to extract, nothing to trust, nothing to maintain separately. That's the condition my whole experiment had to borrow from a benchmark, and Legend just… has it.

Then there's the compiler, which feels like the interesting bit. My correction loop works by handing the model a list of what's provably wrong with its answer. Legend's compiler already produces exactly that, and better than I could — Class Person has no property 'birthDate', type errors, multiplicity violations. A free, exact checker with no LLM judging anything.

Where fine-tuning seems like the harder road: a trained model learns one firm's Legend model, and the next firm has a different one. Models change through SDLC constantly, so it goes stale. And Pure corpora are thin — Legend's niche, there's no Spider-sized pile of training data to draw on. Whereas constraining the decoder to the grammar plus whatever model is currently loaded needs no examples at all and works on a model it's never seen.

Two things I'd poke at if this went anywhere. The eval set may already exist — Legend Services are curated, tested, version-controlled queries sitting in the repo. Turn one into a natural-language question, see if it round-trips. Ground truth is the real query and its real output, so no judge is needed and nobody has to build a benchmark. And entitlements might become a decode-time property: Legend already carries ownership and validation constructs, so if the allowed vocabulary is filtered by the caller's entitlements, the agent can't construct a query over data it shouldn't see. For the FINOS banks that sentence might matter more than any accuracy number.

All speculative. I haven't looked at whether Pure's grammar comes out in a usable shape, and a query can compile fine and still answer the wrong question. But it feels like it wants to work.

6  ·  Poke at how “grounded” actually works

Really just a question. How does the semantic model reach the generation? If it's context assembled into a prompt — the usual answer — then the 0.18-vs-0.79 result applies directly and is cheap to test on your stack. Either it finds headroom or it doesn't, and I'd rather know that before suggesting anything else.

Why I think this fits at all

Short version: the thing my experiment was missing is the thing you already sell.

Every caveat on my numbers comes back to one fact — the graph was handed to me by a benchmark. I never had to build it, so I never proved I could. When I did try extracting one from ordinary enterprise prose, it scored −0.12 against its own source documents, which is a polite way of saying it was worse than nothing. That gap is the entire reason I'd be having this conversation with you rather than with anyone else. A maintained, resolved, governed semantic model is the precondition my results quietly assume, and it's what your customers already live in.

The other half is that the failures I chased weren't where I expected. Retrieval kept turning out to be fine — the right material was there, and the answer still came out wrong. What moved the number was where the knowledge met the generation: same graph, same query, same model, same candidates, 0.18 pasted into a prompt against 0.79 handed back as a diff of errors. That's a stage nobody optimises, sitting downstream of everything everybody does optimise.

And it's all inference-time. No training, no retraining when the model changes, nothing that competes with superalignment or reranking or fine-tuning — it sits after them and picks up what they structurally can't. Which is why I keep landing on “this composes with what they've built” rather than “this replaces it.”

Could easily be that your semantic layer already closes most of this through context, in which case there's not much here and it's a short conversation. But it seems worth an afternoon to find out.

What you'd actually have to give me

One function. That's the whole coupling surface:

supported(question) -> set[str] — the entities the semantic model will vouch for.

Ontology, entity resolution, query execution, row-level access, freshness — all stay exactly where they are. Nothing parses your semantic model, nothing stores triples, nothing needs write access, and no data leaves your environment.

If any of it's interesting

Nothing here is built and I'm not proposing to build anything yet. The sensible next step for any of them is the same: pick one, spend a few days, get a number, and let the number decide whether there's a real thing here.

If it ever got past that, the shape I'd want is three arms on the same questions and model — current retrieval, graph context in the prompt, correction loop — with the scoring agreed before the run. More than zero fixed, exactly zero broken, and significance measured against the prompt-stuffing arm, not the no-graph baseline, since that's the easy comparison and the one that flatters me.

And if it doesn't clear that bar, the honest read is that your semantic layer is already getting most of the value through context and the only thing left is the audit trail. I'd write that up the same way as a win.
